## Awaiting a ruling

### Jeremiah 29:11 — paired with *"I already know how this ends"*
> "For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope."

Spoken to exiles in Babylon, corporate rather than individual, attached to a seventy-year timeline — the same letter tells them to build houses and settle in. Against catastrophic prediction it does work (the point is precisely that they don't know the ending), but it may carry too much baggage to use straight. **Proposed alternative:** Psalm 31:15, *"My times are in your hand"* — first person, said from inside distress rather than promised over it.

### Galatians 1:10 — paired with *"I know what they're thinking about me"*
> "For am I now seeking the approval of man, or of God? Or am I trying to please man? If I were still trying to please man, I would not be a servant of Christ."

Paul defending his apostolic commission against opponents, not counselling someone anxious about a friend's silence. Whole, "I would not be a servant of Christ" lands hard on a teenager reading it about a text message. **Proposed alternative:** Proverbs 29:25, *"The fear of man lays a snare, but whoever trusts in the LORD is safe"* — general address, squarely about the thing.

### Romans 8:1 — used twice
> "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus."

Paired with *"I should be handling this better"* **and** in the "after you've failed" group. The only verse doing double duty — pick one home, or keep both deliberately. Separately, "therefore" is carrying chapters 5–7; standing alone it can read as "don't feel bad," which is not what Paul means.

### 1 Samuel 16:7 — in the "when you're not enough" group
Currently shown as the second half only:
> "For the LORD sees not as man sees: man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart."

**This is the one exception to the whole-verse rule, and it needs a decision.** The complete verse opens with God *rejecting* Eliab — and the reader is already convinced she has been rejected. Either accept the trim (and note the exception), move it, or cut it.

### Micah 7:8 — in the "after you've failed" group
> "Rejoice not over me, O my enemy; when I fall, I shall rise; when I sit in darkness, the LORD will be a light to me."

The speaker is Zion personified, taunting a national enemy, and the fall is judgment for sin. "When I fall, I shall rise" reads beautifully on a bad exam day but carries freight it wasn't packed with — and "O my enemy" is odd when the enemy is your own head.

### Joshua 1:9 — in the "when you need courage" group
> "Have I not commanded you? Be strong and courageous..."

A commissioning for the conquest of Canaan, addressed to Joshua specifically. The most-quoted verse for exams and the furthest from its job. It is also the second "be strong and courageous" in the app, alongside Deuteronomy 31:6.

### Isaiah 1:18 — cut from the app, still worth a ruling
Was in the "ashamed" group; removed in the last rebuild for space. The full ESV makes the setting visible — "Come now, let us reason together" opens a covenant lawsuit, and vv. 19–20 attach conditions. Read as a flat promise of cleansing it is warmer than the passage is. **Restore it, or leave it out?**

### 2 Corinthians 10:12 — also cut, also worth a ruling
Was in the "comparing" group. About rival teachers commending themselves in Corinth; the subject is "they," and "without understanding" aimed at a struggling student is harsher than intended. It also put a second verse from the same polemical passage into the app.

## Patterns worth one decision each

**Corporate promises applied to individuals.** Isaiah 41:10, Isaiah 43:2, Deuteronomy 31:6 and Isaiah 40:31 are all addressed to Israel and read here as promises to a person. Ordinary Christian practice, and the New Testament does the same thing with Deuteronomy 31:6 at Hebrews 13:5. Should the app be transparent about that, or leave it be?

**How much context to show.** Every verse is a single quotation plus a reference. A one-sentence "who this was spoken to and about what" could sit under any of them. It costs nothing visually and it is the honest version — but it also makes the page longer, which cuts against the consolidation work.

**Philippians 4:9 on the grounding screen.** Shown whole, as the rule requires. Its closing clause — "and the God of peace will be with you" — is perfect there. Its first half is Paul saying *imitate me*, which is odd standing alone. Keep both verses whole, or show 4:8 with only that closing line?
